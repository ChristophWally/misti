# Section 8.5: Complete Forms for Defective Verb "vigere" (to be in force)

## Overview
The defective verb "vigere" (to be in force, to be vigorous) demonstrates semantic restrictions in Latin conjugation. This verb is primarily used in third person forms due to its impersonal nature - laws, customs, and conditions "are in force" rather than persons actively "being in force."

**Total Limited Forms**: ~67 forms (significantly reduced from standard ~200+ forms)
**Primary Restrictions**: 
- Semantic: Laws/customs are in force (3rd person focus)
- Historical: Limited attestation in classical sources
- Impersonal nature: Primarily used with neuter subjects

## Passive Forms - LARGELY NON-EXISTENT

### Note on Passive Forms
The verb "vigere" is primarily intransitive, meaning "to be in force" or "to flourish." Passive forms are semantically inappropriate and historically unattested.


## Summary of Restrictions

### Semantic Restrictions
1. **Impersonal Nature**: Laws, customs, conditions are "in force" - not persons
2. **Intransitive**: No direct object, eliminating passive constructions
3. **State Description**: Describes condition rather than action

### Historical Restrictions
1. **Limited Attestation**: Classical sources primarily use 3rd person forms
2. **Technical Usage**: Primarily legal/administrative contexts
3. **Formal Register**: Not common in colloquial speech

### Form Count Analysis
- **Standard Verb**: ~200+ forms
- **Vigere (Defective)**: ~67 forms
- **Reduction**: ~67% fewer forms due to semantic/historical constraints

### Database Architecture Impact
This demonstrates how semantic constraints create natural boundaries in verb conjugation systems, requiring flexible database design to handle:
- Missing form patterns
